Have you ever met a software developer that made the assertion, "We do Agile," when you asked them what software development process their team follows? Besides being grossly wrong from a grammatical perspective, the statement also belies a fundamental misunderstanding of software development methodologies that is rife within the software development industry. In this article I'm going to give a brief overview of the history of software development methodologies and what I *think* the best course is for teams and organizations.

## Waterfall Methodology

Every team or organization that produces software uses some sort of [software development life cycle (SDLC)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systems_development_life_cycle). When SDLCs originated in the 1960s, they were initially called Systems Development Life Cycles and encompassed the creation and maintenance of large information systems that provided the core business functionality for companies. Many of the early definitions of SDLCs will be familiar to engineers in other engineering disciplines since they have to follow a very prescriptive design process or softare developers that are familiar with the Waterfall model for software development.

In the Waterfall model, software development is broken into a number of distinct, linear, sequential phases that produce an end softare product. This methodology was outlined by Winston W. Royce in a 1970 article which articulated, among other things, how this model had major flaws and, essentially, should not be used. As is often the case, no one really seemed to read the paper and the Waterfall methodology became the predominant model for software development. To the surprise of no one, it was even adopted as the United States Department of Defense software development standard in 1985. (I've worked on a number of projects early in my career for the DoD that followed this methodology.)
