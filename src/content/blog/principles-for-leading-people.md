Leading people is a complex task.

That is because humans are complex. We all have needs, desires, hopes, fears, and expectations. Bring all of these subjective elements of past experiences together in a group of humans to achieve a goal, and you have a milieu of complexity on your hands.

This is where a skilled leader truly shows value. Leaders have to be focused on both the individual and the group to achieve goals.

## Levels of Leadership

Because the job changes by level, I first need to clarify what kind of leader I'm talking about.

Are we talking about a first-line manager whose job is to lead individual contributors, like a team of software developers?

Are we talking about a senior manager or director, whose job is to translate strategy into organizational execution, like a person overseeing multiple teams of developers building a product?

Are we talking about an executive, whose job is to define organizational strategy to achieve overall business objectives, like a VP of Engineering who drives what product is being built and what organizational success looks like?

Each of these leadership roles is different and has different role, but I'm going to focus on the first-line manager. The core principles for leading people effectively at that level also hold at higher levels of leadership; they simply show up in different ways and at different scales.

# Categorizing Core Principles

I have nine core principles for leading people, grouped into three categories: direction, development, and environment. These are the lenses I use to think about what good leadership looks like.

Directional principles deal with people understanding their role and how they fit into an organization. Without direction, people feel aimless and disconnected from the organization because they do not know how their current work matters.

Developmental principles deal with how a person is progressing in their career. Without development, a person will lack key skill growth, feel stuck in their job, and never be capable of taking on progressively harder work.

Environmental principles deal with how easy it is for people to do great work. Without a solid environment, people feel blocked from doing their best work and generally resign themselves to mediocrity. In other words, even good people struggle when the system around them is misaligned.

If a leader does not focus on all three categories of principles, employees will start to get frustrated, and ultimately they will vote with their feet and leave either the team or the company. The maxim holds true: people do not leave jobs; they leave leaders.

## Directional Principles

Every person on a team is doing something, but is that something valuable? Directional principles help people work on what matters and give them the psychological feeling of being part of a larger group. This is where a leader creates clarity before asking for execution.

These principles are:

- Define role clarity and the outcomes for success
- Connect day-to-day work to the team's goals
- Hold high standards with fair accountability

Defining role clarity and the outcomes for success for a role is incredibly important. This helps people understand what their role actually is. For many people, the only time they get clarity on their role is when they press a hiring manager during an interview. After that, they rarely hear anything about what their role on a team actually is.

If you start hearing "what am I supposed to be working on?" or "is this my job?" during a one-on-one meeting, you should immediately realize that role clarity is lacking. And if it is lacking for one person on the team, it is probably lacking for everyone.

The second directional principle follows the first: connect day-to-day work to the team's goals. If someone understands their role but does not understand how their current work aligns with the team's goals, that means you have a clarity issue. It could be that this person does not understand the team's goals, or it could be that you have not explicitly stated them.

At every team meeting I hold, I always walk through two things: our overarching team objective and the goals we have to meet to achieve that objective. I want to ensure everyone on the team has clarity about what we are doing and why we are doing it.

The last directional principle is simple: hold high standards with fair accountability. Everyone on a team needs to know whether they are meeting the mark or not. They also need to know how their role is actually being tracked. You cannot meet a goal if you do not know how it is being measured or what success looks like.

Are there regular product deadlines that need to be hit, and does the team know what those are? Are there metrics being tracked for customer satisfaction or revenue targets? Do you have pull request close-time SLAs that everyone needs to help achieve? Everyone needs to know what the team, and what each individual, is accountable for so they are fairly assessed on their work.

When it comes down to it, directional principles are really about one thing: clarity. The role of a leader is to bring clarity to members of the team. This means removing ambiguity, making decisions, and continuing to communicate so that each person on the team is clear on the direction the team is heading.

## Development Principles

Each person on a team is somewhere in their career journey. Maybe they are a principal engineer defining the technology and architecture for an entire area, or maybe they just graduated from college and have no idea what is going on. In both cases, and every step in between, team members need to understand where they are and have clear direction on how to develop in their careers. These are developmental principles.

These principles are:

- Align work with career direction
- Communicate with candor, respect, and care
- Recognize meaningful progress and impact

When work is aligned to where someone wants to go in their career, motivation changes. People stop seeing tasks as random requests and start seeing them as reps that build capability.

For example, if an engineer wants to grow into a staff role, they should not just be assigned more tickets. They should be given opportunities to drive technical direction, influence across team boundaries, and mentor other developers. If someone wants to grow into management, they need opportunities to lead initiatives, run meetings, and practice giving feedback. Development is not accidental; it requires intentional work assignment.

The second development principle is communication with candor, respect, and care. As commonly expressed in the [Radical Candor framework](https://www.radicalcandor.com/) by Kim Scott, "Candor without care is cruelty. Care without candor is avoidance." Great managers do both at the same time, demonstrating respect for the team member.

If someone is doing great work, tell them directly and specifically. If someone is missing expectations, tell them directly and specifically. Ambiguous feedback helps no one. Most people can handle hard feedback when they trust that it is being shared to help them grow.

The third development principle is to recognize meaningful progress and impact. Recognition does not need to be dramatic. In fact, simple and specific recognition is usually more effective.

"You handled that customer escalation calmly and reduced risk for the entire release" is useful recognition. "Great job" is not. The goal is to reinforce the behaviors that matter and help people see the connection between their effort and team outcomes.

## Environmental Principles

Even highly capable people with clear direction can underperform in a poor environment. This is why the environment is a core leadership responsibility.

These principles are:

- Remove systemic blockers so people can do their best work
- Build trust through consistency and follow-through
- Create psychological safety for questions, dissent, and learning from mistakes

Removing blockers is one of the most visible ways leaders create value. Sometimes blockers are local, like unclear requirements or too many meetings. Sometimes they are systemic, like cross-team dependencies, broken processes, or organizational bureaucracy.

Strong first-line leaders remove what they can directly and escalate what they cannot. If you cannot remove a blocker, you still own making progress on it visible so your team knows they are being advocated for.

Trust is built through consistency and follow-through. If you say you will do something, do it. If priorities change, explain why. If a decision is made, communicate it clearly and repeatedly. Teams lose trust quickly when communication is inconsistent or commitments disappear.

Psychological safety is the final environmental principle. People need to be able to ask "basic" questions, challenge ideas, admit mistakes, and propose alternatives without fear of being punished or humiliated.

This does not mean standards are lowered or accountability goes away. It means the team can tell the truth quickly, which is the foundation of high performance.

## Putting It All Together

When I step back, these nine principles form a simple leadership operating system:

- Set direction
- Grow people
- Shape the environment

If direction is weak, people drift.

If development is weak, people stagnate.

If the environment is weak, people disengage.

Great leaders pay attention to all three categories at the same time and adjust as the team evolves.

Leading people will always be complex because people are complex. But complexity does not mean mystery. If you consistently apply these principles, you will build stronger teams, healthier culture, and better outcomes over time.
